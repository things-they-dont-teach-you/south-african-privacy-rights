# Lesson 1: What counts as your personal information
<!-- facts: Your rights under POPIA; What counts -->

## By the end, the learner can

- Say what POPIA is and who the Information Regulator is.
- Recognise personal information, including the less obvious kinds.
- Name the six reasons an organisation may use their information, and say which one consent is.
- Explain what withdrawing consent and objecting do.

## Key ideas

**POPIA** (the Protection of Personal Information Act) sets the rules for how organisations
collect, store, use and share information about you. The **Information Regulator** enforces it.

**You are the "data subject".** The organisation deciding what happens to your information is the
**"responsible party"**: your bank, your cellphone network, a shop's loyalty programme, your
school, a government department.

**Personal information is far more than your name and ID number.** It includes your phone number,
email, address and location, your health, financial and employment history, your opinions, your
private messages, what other people say about you, and your fingerprints or face scan. The full
list is in `facts.md`.

**"Special" personal information** (health, religion, race, politics, sex life, biometrics,
criminal records) gets extra protection.

**An organisation needs a lawful reason.** POPIA allows six (`facts.md`). Consent is only one of
them. A bank also processes your details because of its contract with you and because laws make
it check who you are. So "I never agreed" doesn't always mean "they're breaking the law".

**Consent can be withdrawn.** Where they rely on your consent, you can withdraw it at any time.
Where they rely on their "legitimate interest", you can object on reasonable grounds, and once you
object they must stop, unless a law requires the processing.

**Not covered:** what you do in your own household, like a family WhatsApp group.

## Worked example

Lindiwe signs up for a supermarket loyalty card. To give her points, the shop needs her name and
phone number: that's the contract. Then it starts sharing her shopping habits with an insurance
company. That's a new use she didn't sign up for. She can ask what the shop shares and with whom
(lesson 4), and object or withdraw consent (lesson 2).

## Exercise

Ask the learner to list three organisations that hold information about them, and for each, guess
which of the six reasons it relies on. Then ask: which of those could they withdraw from tomorrow?
